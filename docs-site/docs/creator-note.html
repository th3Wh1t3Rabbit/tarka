A note from the creator

A note from the creator

The email that changed the week

I had been using Nansen for a while, across several larger private projects, already going through many thousands of credits a day. Then I woke up, opened an email about the Meridian Buildathon, and realised there were roughly thirteen days left to submit something.

I could not open-source my existing work for the competition. I also had no idea, when I woke up that morning—or even when I first read the email—that I was about to put everything else aside and make a Nansen investigation game. That decision happened quickly.

My first game, from scratch

I considered a lot of ideas and wanted to attempt something ambitious for the time available. The early point-and-click adventures I grew up playing offered the right combination of exploration, personality, absurdity, and discovery.

I have been a software engineer for more than twenty years, but I had never actually made a game. There was no half-finished adventure to dust off, no existing pixel-art collection, and no prebuilt cast waiting in a folder. The game and its original assets were created for this project. I chose the genre and wrote the script myself, including the exchanges, jokes, and little pauses that make Rook and Arthur’s relationship work.

A different way to see the data

Nansen had to be essential, not a logo on an unrelated project. I wanted the API data to drive the investigation while the characters introduced its possibilities naturally. Rook could learn alongside players who were new to crypto; people already familiar with it could enjoy the more particular jokes and references.

The terminal was also an experiment in how we think. Instead of starting with raw rows and every available control, start with a question. Build the conditions from clues. Explain what a record shows—and what it does not—in that context. Keep the underlying detail close enough to inspect.

I deliberately gave that experiment only 393 × 219 logical pixels inside the monitor bezel. Some screen space was sacrificed to the game’s nostalgic presentation. That was the challenge: not another modern web app with more panels and dropdowns, but a small, readable instrument for directed inquiry. I hoped it might inspire someone to reconsider their own dashboards, summaries, or ways of exploring data.

Making the deadline

This was built for Nansen and this competition from scratch. It involved a few all-nighters, constant decisions about scope, and plenty of doubt about whether I could finish in time. More interactions, details, and ideas were left on the cutting-room floor than I would have liked. Some choices were compromises; others had to be cuts.

I am not presenting it as the perfect adventure game, or claiming perfect code and architecture. With more planning and development time I would polish many parts substantially. The goal under these constraints was to ship something stable, reliable, understandable, and hopefully genuinely fun.

Thanks for playing

For readers who want to inspect the writing itself, the Complete Script Explorer is a spoiler-filled map of all 724 final script bubbles, 1,505 runtime routes, and 214 executable action fixtures in the shipped game.

I hope Tarka works on several levels: an entertaining old-school adventure, a gentle introduction to onchain investigation, and a demonstration of what Nansen makes possible when you ask useful questions. Building it was a very fun and educational adventure of its own.

Getting it across the line in time feels like a good moment to rest and catch up on sleep. Thanks for spending some of your time in the Records Office. I hope you enjoy the company—even if Arthur is slow to say the same.
